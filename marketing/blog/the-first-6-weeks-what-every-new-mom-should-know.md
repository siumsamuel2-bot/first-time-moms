## Introduction: Welcome to the Fourth Trimester

You've read the books, taken the classes, and packed the hospital bag. But now you're home — and no one warned you about *this*.

The first six weeks after delivery, often called the "fourth trimester," are a period of intense physical recovery, hormonal chaos, and emotional adjustment. For first-time moms especially, this phase can feel like running a marathon you didn't know you signed up for.

At Firstweeks, we believe that informed recovery is faster recovery. This guide breaks down what to expect week by week, when to call for help, and how to give yourself the grace you deserve.

---

## What to Expect Week by Week

### Week 1: Hospital to Home

The first week is a blur of feedings, diaper changes, and learning what your baby needs. But your body is also doing serious work:

- **Uterine involution**: Your uterus is shrinking back to its pre-pregnancy size. You may feel cramping, especially during breastfeeding.
- **Bleeding (lochia)**: Heavy bleeding is normal for the first few days. It should gradually turn from red to pink to yellow-white.
- **Perineal or incision pain**: Whether you had a vaginal delivery or C-section, your body needs time to heal. Use ice packs, pain relievers (as directed by your doctor), and rest.
- **Breast changes**: Your milk may come in around days 3–5. Expect engorgement, leaking, and possibly sore nipples.

**Pro tip**: Sleep when the baby sleeps. I know everyone says this, but it's real. Even 20-minute naps help.

---

### Weeks 2–3: The Reality Sets In

By week two, the adrenaline of birth has worn off. This is when many moms feel the weight of it all.

- **Emotional shifts**: You may feel weepy, anxious, or overwhelmed. This is often the "baby blues" — hormonal and normal. But if feelings persist or worsen, it's important to talk to someone.
- **Physical recovery continues**: Bleeding should be lighter. If it suddenly increases, call your doctor.
- **Feeding routines**: You're likely feeding every 2–3 hours. This is exhausting. Try to build small routines that give you mental breaks.
- **Sleep deprivation**: You may feel like a zombie. This is the time to ask for help unapologetically.

**Red flag**: If you have a fever over 100.4°F, foul-smelling discharge, or severe abdominal pain, call your OB-GYN immediately.

---

### Weeks 4–6: Finding Your Rhythm

By week four, many moms start to feel slightly more human. Your body is continuing to heal, and you're learning your baby's cues.

- **Postpartum check-up**: Most OB-GYNs schedule a 6-week check-up. This is your time to ask *every* question — no matter how small.
- **Exercise**: If cleared by your doctor, you can start gentle walks, pelvic floor exercises, and light stretching.
- **Mental health**: If you're still feeling down, anxious, or disconnected, tell your doctor. Postpartum depression can appear anytime in the first year.
- **Intimacy**: Many couples struggle with physical intimacy during this time. It's normal. Communicate openly and be patient with yourself.

## Red Flags: When to Call Your Doctor

Call your OB-GYN or go to the ER if you experience:
- Heavy bleeding (soaking a pad in less than an hour)
- Fever over 100.4°F
- Severe headache or vision changes
- Chest pain or difficulty breathing
- Severe abdominal pain or a swollen, tender uterus
- Signs of a blood clot (pain, warmth, or swelling in one leg)
- Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby

**If you ever feel unsafe, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room.**

## Self-Care Isn't Selfish

New mothers are often expected to put everyone else first. But your recovery is the foundation of everything else.

- **Eat well**: Focus on iron, protein, and hydration. Your body is healing.
- **Rest**: Even if you can't sleep, rest. Lie down, breathe, and let your nervous system calm down.
- **Accept help**: If someone offers to hold the baby, do laundry, or bring food — say yes.

## The Bottom Line

The first 6 weeks are hard. Really hard. But they are also temporary. With the right information, support, and self-compassion, you can navigate them with more confidence and less fear.

You don't need to do this perfectly. You just need to do what's right for you and your baby — one day at a time.
